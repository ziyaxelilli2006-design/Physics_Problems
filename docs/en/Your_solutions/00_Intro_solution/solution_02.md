
## 2. Systems of Equations

**The Setup:**

1. $2x + 3y = 12$
2. $x - y = 1$

### The Strategy: The Substitution Method

While there are many ways to solve this (like Elimination or Graphing), **Substitution** is often the most intuitive. It involves turning one equation into a "key" that unlocks the other.

#### Step 1: Isolate one variable

Take the simpler equation (Equation 2) and solve for $x$ in terms of $y$.


$$x - y = 1$$


Add $y$ to both sides:


$$x = y + 1$$


Now we have a "definition" for $x$. Wherever we see $x$ in the other equation, we can replace it with $(y + 1)$.

#### Step 2: Substitute into the first equation

Plug our new definition of $x$ into Equation 1:


$$2(y + 1) + 3y = 12$$

#### Step 3: Solve for $y$

Now we have an equation with only one variable. Let’s do the math:

1. **Distribute the 2:** $2y + 2 + 3y = 12$
2. **Combine like terms ($2y$ and $3y$):** $5y + 2 = 12$
3. **Subtract 2 from both sides:** $5y = 10$
4. **Divide by 5:** $\mathbf{y = 2}$

#### Step 4: Solve for $x$

Now that we know $y = 2$, we go back to our "key" from Step 1:


$$x = y + 1$$

$$x = 2 + 1$$

$$\mathbf{x = 3}$$

---

### The Geometric Interpretation

If you were to draw these two equations on a standard grid, each would form a straight line. The point where those lines physically cross is exactly $(3, 2)$.

### Verification (The "Double Check")

Always plug your answers back into the original equations to be $100\%$ sure:

* **Eq 1:** $2(3) + 3(2) = 6 + 6 = 12$ (Correct!)
* **Eq 2:** $3 - 2 = 1$ (Correct!)

**Final Result:**
The values that satisfy both equations are **$x = 3$** and **$y = 2$**.
